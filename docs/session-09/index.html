    Session 9 — Sniffing, MITM, Social Engineering & DoS

    The network and the human. Read the wire, sit in the middle, trick the person, take the service down — the three surfaces that surround the application.

    Session 8 was the application's own code. Session 9 is everything around it — the network it rides on and the people who use it. Three surfaces, each with its own precondition, its own visible effect, and its own control.

    You will read traffic straight off a switched LAN with sniffing, rewrite who-is-who with ARP poisoning to sit in the middle of a connection, strip the padlock off HTTPS with SSL-strip, trick the person with social engineering, and exhaust a service with denial-of-service — then carry every one of them to how a defender detects and stops it.

    ⏱ ~4 hours◆ animated diagrams + prove-it labs🎓 every attack mapped to MITRE ATT&CK
    How this session runs

    We take the surfaces left to right — wire, human, availability — each with theory, an animated diagram that plays, and the detection a SOC analyst would actually write. Then a defence block ties them together and a capstone closes the session.

    S9 · P02Method · reading the wire⏱ 7 min

    Sniffing — reading traffic in transit

    What a host can actually see on a switched network — and the ARP trick that lets the attacker see it all.

    The idea

    A network card normally drops frames that are not addressed to it. Put it in promiscuous mode and it keeps everything it hears — every cleartext protocol becomes readable. On a hub that is the whole LAN; on a modern switch you only see your own traffic, so the attacker first has to make the traffic come to them (ARP poisoning, next page).

    Key takeaway

    Switching is not security. Sniffing is passive and silent on the wire — there is no packet to detect. You stop it by removing the cleartext (TLS, SSH) and by catching the active step that feeds a switched network to the sniffer: ARP anomalies and NICs in promiscuous mode. · MITRE T1040.

    S9 · P03Method · becoming the middle⏱ 8 min

    ARP poisoning — becoming the man in the middle

    How a local attacker rewrites who-is-who on the LAN and slips onto the path in both directions.

    The idea

    ARP has no authentication: a host believes the last reply it hears. The attacker sends forged replies to both the victim and the gateway — “the other side’s IP is at my MAC” — and both caches now point at the attacker. Every packet in both directions is relayed through them: a full man-in-the-middle.

    Key takeaway

    Poison the cache, own the path. One forged reply to each side turns a switched LAN into a shared one — for the attacker alone. Detect it by the duplicate-MAC / changed-MAC signature and defend with Dynamic ARP Inspection + DHCP snooping. · MITRE T1557.002.

    S9 · P04Method · stripping the padlock⏱ 6 min

    SSL stripping — turning HTTPS back into HTTP

    How an on-path attacker removes the padlock — and why HSTS makes it impossible.

    The idea

    Once on the path, the attacker keeps a real HTTPS session to the site but hands the victim an ordinary HTTP copy — same page, no padlock. The user rarely notices “Not secure,” so the login is typed in cleartext straight to the attacker. HSTS is the fix: the browser forces HTTPS itself, so there is no HTTP request left to downgrade.

    Key takeaway

    The site stays HTTPS; only your side is downgraded. SSL-strip needs an existing MITM and a user who starts over HTTP. HSTS (and the preload list) remove the HTTP request the attack depends on; serve HTTPS-only with secure redirects. · MITRE T1557.

    S9 · P05Why · the psychology⏱ 6 min

    Social engineering — why people say yes

    Attacks on the person, not the firewall. Six psychological levers that move a target from checking to clicking.

    The idea

    Social engineering skips the firewall and targets the person. The attacker borrows authority, imposes urgency, raises fear, dangles reward, baits curiosity, or hides behind a familiar brand — each lever pushing the target to react before they reason.

    Key takeaway

    Urgency is itself a red flag. The message is engineered to move you off slow, checking thought onto fast, emotional reaction. You cannot patch a human — so build a process that catches the click: verify sensitive requests out-of-band, and back it with MFA, mail filtering and least privilege. · MITRE T1566.

    S9 · P06Method · reading the headers⏱ 8 min

    Anatomy of a phishing email

    The same message in two views — the brand the victim sees, and the headers that give it away.

    The idea

    A phishing email is two things at once: what the victim sees (a trusted brand, an urgent threat) and what the analyst reads (headers that expose a spoofed sender). Triage is the habit of reading the second view before trusting the first.

    Key takeaway

    Read the address, not the display name; hover every link. The look-alike domain, the mismatched Reply-To, and failing SPF/DKIM/DMARC are the ground truth. The body is designed to rush you; the headers do not lie. · MITRE T1566.002.

    S9 · P07Method · SPF / DKIM / DMARC⏱ 9 min

    Email authentication — SPF, DKIM, DMARC

    The three DNS-backed checks that catch a spoofed sender before it reaches the inbox.

    The idea

    Three checks sit between a sender and the inbox. SPF asks “is this IP allowed to send for the domain?” DKIM asks “is the message signed by the domain's key and unaltered?” and DMARC ties them to the visible From domain and decides what to do on failure — all read from DNS the attacker cannot forge.

    Key takeaway

    A spoof fails all three. Wrong IP (SPF), no valid signature (DKIM), no alignment (DMARC) — and a p=reject policy drops it before delivery. Publish SPF, sign with DKIM, and move DMARC to p=reject once monitoring is clean. · MITRE T1566.

    S9 · P08Taxonomy · the variants⏱ 6 min

    The phishing family — spear, whaling, vishing, smishing

    One technique, five disguises — sorted by channel and how much the attacker researched you.

    The idea

    One technique wears many disguises. The difference is channel (email, voice, SMS) and how much the attacker researched the target — from mass phishing sprayed at thousands to whaling aimed at a single executive.

    Key takeaway

    The more targeted, the more convincing. Filters catch mass phishing; spear phishing, whaling, vishing and smishing use real names, context and off-email channels to reach a human directly. Same defence everywhere: authenticate mail, verify out-of-band, MFA, and easy reporting. · MITRE T1566.

    S9 · P09Method · the cloned page⏱ 7 min

    Credential harvesting — the cloned login page

    How a fake login captures the password and hands the victim to the real site — and why MFA breaks it.

    The idea

    Credential harvesting turns a click into a captured password. The attacker clones a real login page, hosts it on a look-alike domain, and — after the victim submits — forwards them to the genuine site so nothing seems wrong. Shown here for defensive understanding only, with fictional accounts.

    Key takeaway

    A stolen password is only enough without MFA. The clone's only tells are the URL and the missing second factor. Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/passkeys) is bound to the real domain, so it will not authenticate to the clone; a password manager won't autofill the wrong domain either. · MITRE T1566 / T1056.003.

    S9 · P10Overview · the three families⏱ 6 min

    Denial of service — the three families

    DoS vs DDoS, and whether the attack drowns the bandwidth, the connection table, or the application.

    The idea

    A denial-of-service attack doesn't steal data — it makes a service unavailable. It comes from one source (DoS) or a botnet of many (DDoS), and it works by exhausting one of three resources: bandwidth, the connection table, or the application itself. The category decides the defence.

    Key takeaway

    Classify by what it exhausts. Volumetric floods the pipe (scrub upstream / CDN), protocol attacks fill state (SYN cookies), application attacks drain workers (timeouts + WAF). Know your traffic baseline so you can tell a spike from an attack. · MITRE T1498 / T1499.

    S9 · P11Method · state exhaustion⏱ 7 min

    SYN flood — exhausting the connection table

    How spoofed half-open handshakes fill the backlog so real clients are refused — and why SYN cookies stop it.

    The idea

    Every TCP connection opens with a three-way handshake. A SYN flood sends thousands of SYNs from spoofed addresses and never completes the handshake, so the server holds thousands of half-open connections until its backlog is full — and real clients are refused. It's a state attack, not a bandwidth one.

    Key takeaway

    It exhausts state, not bandwidth. The fix removes the state the attack relies on: SYN cookies allocate nothing until a valid ACK returns, so unanswered SYNs cost the server nothing. Add rate-limiting, firewalls, and upstream scrubbing for the DDoS version. · MITRE T1499.002.

    S9 · P12Method · the trickle attack⏱ 6 min

    Slow HTTP — Slowloris and the trickle attack

    How a few slow connections tie up the whole worker pool at almost no bandwidth.

    The idea

    Slowloris is the opposite of a flood: it opens many connections and sends each HTTP request one header at a time, very slowly, never finishing it. The server keeps a worker reserved for each, so a few hundred near-idle connections tie up the whole pool at almost no bandwidth.

    Key takeaway

    The resource it drains is concurrent connections. Because each connection completes the handshake and sends a valid partial request, it looks legitimate and slips past flood defences. Stop it with request/header timeouts, per-IP connection limits, and an event-driven reverse proxy or CDN/WAF that buffers the full request first. · MITRE T1499.002.

    S9 · P13SOC view · detection⏱ 8 min

    Detection — what the SOC watches across the surfaces

    Every attack in this session leaves a signal — the indicator, the tool, and the ATT&CK technique for each surface.

    The idea

    Every attack in this session leaves a signal a SOC can alert on. Detection is the habit of knowing the normal baseline for each surface — ARP mappings, mail authentication, connection rates — and firing when reality deviates from it, then triaging the alert to a true/false positive mapped to ATT&CK.

    Key takeaway

    Each surface has its own indicator. Wire → changed MAC / promiscuous NIC; Human → failed SPF/DKIM/DMARC and look-alike domains; Availability → SYN_RECV and connection-rate anomalies. The loop never changes: baseline, alert, triage, respond. · MITRE T1040 / T1557 / T1566 / T1498 / T1499.

    S9 · P14Controls · prevent / detect / respond⏱ 7 min

    Defence in depth — prevent, detect, respond

    No single control is enough. Layer prevention, detection and response across every surface.

    The idea

    No single control stops an attack on its own, so defence is layered. For each surface you prevent (make it fail), detect (see it when it happens anyway), and respond (contain and recover). Prevention lowers the odds, detection buys time, response limits the blast radius.

    Key takeaway

    Assume one layer fails. TLS and DAI, DMARC and MFA, SYN cookies and rate-limits stop most of it; monitoring catches the rest; and a tested response plan keeps one failure from becoming a breach. Defence in depth is a system, not a single wall.
